Top stories

1. France burns over fuel prices, and Europe’s right cashes in

Grievances over high fuel prices exploded into rioting and looting across France over the weekend, plunging Emmanuel Macron’s final months in office into turmoil. Paris has deployed troops ahead of demonstrations planned for Saturday; PM Sébastien Lecornu said “targeted” curfews helped bring a calmer Monday night, with local authorities deciding whether to extend them. Insurers are bracing for losses as the riots become “like natural catastrophes.”

Touring the Gulf, Macron announced an extra 200,000 tonnes of monthly diesel deliveries to France. Politically, the first presidential poll since the unrest shows the race barely budged: Marine Le Pen remains out front, with Jean-Luc Mélenchon in a close fight for second. Across Europe, rightist parties are using the fuel crunch to press governments in Germany, Italy and Poland to restore energy ties with Russia (“I love the smell of Russian gas in the morning,” a Polish far-right leader said). In the US, Republicans facing tough midterms are pointing to France as a warning of what a Democratic win would bring.

Why it matters: An energy-price shock is turning into a political one: it strengthens Le Pen six months before France votes, weakens Europe’s resolve on Russian energy sanctions and adds fiscal pressure on an already indebted French state.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (21 Oct) · Reuters Daily Briefing · Politico Brussels Playbook · Politico Playbook Capitals · Bloomberg Money Stuff

2. French bond selloff stokes eurozone contagion fears

French government bonds sold off again, pushing the spread between French and German 10-year yields to its widest since 2012 as investors demand a bigger premium for Paris’s debt. Morningstar analysts said the demonstrations make it harder to close France’s budget gap, while Commerzbank warned “this is not just idiosyncratic French selling, but [reflects] concerns about a global debt crisis.”

The backdrop: a huge debt burden, the prospect of a far-right president, and a global bond market where the US 10-year yield is above 5%.

Why it matters: France is the eurozone’s second-largest economy; a disorderly widening of its spreads would test the ECB’s tools, lift borrowing costs across the periphery (Italy included) and weigh on the euro.

Sources: Semafor Business · Semafor Flagship (20 Oct)

3. US–Ukraine rupture deepens as Witkoff heads to Putin

Elon Musk ordered the Starlink slowdown that hindered Kyiv’s defence last week, US officials told The Wall Street Journal. Ukraine had also accused Washington of cutting intelligence sharing (which the US denied) and The Washington Post reported the US has held up Patriot interceptor transfers; on Tuesday Kyiv said intelligence sharing and early warnings of Russian strikes had resumed. The US has also struck a diesel deal with Moscow and waived sanctions on buyers of Russian oil and gas.

Trump repeated that Ukraine should “get a new president” and said he was open to meeting Putin and Xi; Zelenskyy has replied that Trump has an affinity for autocrats. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas accused the US of pushing Kyiv toward surrender, while Marco Rubio insisted Washington had not deliberately withheld help. After talks in Oslo (attended for the EU by von der Leyen’s chief of staff Bjoern Seibert), envoy Steve Witkoff is travelling to St. Petersburg to offer Putin a three-way summit with Trump and Zelenskyy. The outcome is pending.

Why it matters: Washington is trading Ukraine’s security for cheaper fuel and a ceasefire push; that shifts the military and financial burden onto Europe and raises the risk of a settlement on Moscow’s terms.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (21 Oct) · Semafor Flagship (20 Oct) · Politico Brussels Playbook · Reuters Daily Briefing

4. Gulf braces for renewed war with Iran; Houthis hit Saudi Arabia

Western and Russian leaders flooded the Gulf with diplomacy that experts fear signals more conflict: the US defence secretary was in Israel, where the US and Israel are reportedly discussing resuming strikes on Iran, while Russia’s foreign minister pledged support for Tehran’s “just cause” and Iran is strengthening its defences. Traders faded a Reuters report of an imminent Iran peace deal, and a further US carrier group (USS Theodore Roosevelt) is due in the region by late November.

Iran-backed Houthi strikes on Saudi airports and energy infrastructure led Riyadh to call off its flagship Future Investment Initiative; the UK will send fighter jets to protect Saudi cities. Riyadh is also retrenching on spending, including steps toward a possible sale of a stake in Al Nassr to RedBird (about $267m). Oil held near a five-month high.

Why it matters: Any resumption of US–Israeli strikes would hit already-tight energy supplies, keeping oil above $100 and feeding the inflation that is pushing central banks toward hikes.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (20 Oct) · Semafor Flagship (21 Oct) · Semafor Business · Polymarket Polygraph

5. Wall Street hedges the AI boom as credit cracks show

Investors are racing to buy credit default swaps against the AI build-out: more than $9bn of CDS tied to Oracle, SpaceX, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Broadcom and Nvidia bonds traded each week in September, up from under $2bn in July (Morgan Stanley, via the FT). Five-year Oracle CDS that cost about 0.33% a year five years ago now cost about 3.3%. Bloomberg’s Matt Levine argues the risk is systematic: if 2030s data-center demand disappoints, the loans all sour together, and the natural insurers are already long the same risk.

Signs of strain: CoreWeave bond yields have topped 15% (comparable SpaceX bonds yield about 7%); Korea’s Elice sank about 30% on its debut; Australia’s Firmus pulled its IPO; and Nscale, in talks to sell about $45bn of compute to Anthropic, lost over $1bn on $140m revenue in H1 while some investors think a $16–19bn valuation, not the $30bn it hopes for, would be an easier sell. Meta and Tesla report this week, offering clarity on spending and financing.

Why it matters: AI-related debt is becoming a market-wide exposure (KKR estimates a fifth of the investment-grade index could be exposed). Rising hedging costs and failed listings suggest the financing window is narrowing, which could slow capex and hit the companies most dependent on borrowed money.

Sources: Bloomberg Money Stuff · Semafor Flagship (20 Oct) · Finimize · Semafor Business

6. Trump hits record-low approval two weeks before the midterms

Trump’s approval fell to a record low in a Reuters/Ipsos poll as Americans worry about high gasoline prices before the 3 November congressional elections. Money is pouring in: Elon Musk has given more than $360m to Republicans (the largest individual donor in modern US history) and pledged SpaceX shares to every American child, while Michael Bloomberg is giving $80m to Democrats. Trump is offering funds for farmers hit by diesel costs and $5,000 checks if Republicans hold Congress. Polymarket notes every statewide Republican in Ohio is in deep trouble.

Why it matters: A Democratic House or Senate would constrain Trump’s fiscal and trade agenda in 2027; his pre-election giveaways and fuel-price diplomacy (Russia, India, Venezuela) are shaping energy markets now.

Sources: Reuters Daily Briefing · Semafor Flagship (21 Oct) · Polymarket Polygraph · Politico Brussels Playbook

Business & markets

• SpaceX blows past estimates. Starlink revenue jumped 69% and the AI unit (“Super Intelligence”, data-center rental and Grok) grew almost tenfold, overtaking Starlink, turning a $1.1bn loss into a $4.4bn profit. It has $90bn in cash and $28bn of annual compute deals with Anthropic, Google and Reflection AI. Lockups on about 35% of shares expire in the coming days.

Why it matters: Cheap funding is a competitive weapon in the neocloud race, but insider selling after the lockup could pressure the shares short term.

Sources: Finimize · Semafor Business · Semafor Flagship (20 Oct)

• The AI boom is now an inflation problem. Fed officials increasingly see AI spending driving inflation; Chicago Fed’s Austan Goolsbee says it is “jumping out of its lane.” Big Tech keeps borrowing as rates rise, so the Fed may have to cool the rest of the economy harder. The 10-year Treasury slipped to 5.096% as September homebuilding fell and pending sales hit their lowest since 2001.

Why it matters: Rate hikes that do not slow AI capex fall on housing and consumers instead; markets already price a possible December hike.

Sources: Reuters Daily Briefing · Semafor Business · Finimize

• Anthropic’s IPO repriced; OpenAI moves first. Polymarket traders no longer see a $2tn market cap as the base case for Anthropic’s IPO, citing corporate cutbacks and more open-source use. Reuters says OpenAI is seeking about $30bn at a valuation above $1.4tn ahead of Anthropic’s expected mid-November debut.

Why it matters: The pricing of the two largest AI listings will set the tone for the whole AI equity and credit complex.

Sources: Polymarket Polygraph · Finimize

• China still buys American chips while walls go up. Alibaba, ByteDance and Tencent have ordered more than $35bn of Nvidia chips (Reuters). Washington banned DeepSeek from most government systems, while Beijing proposed export controls on its most powerful AI models (over 2 trillion parameters or top-10 ranked) and on chipmaking, biotech and solar tech.

Why it matters: Mutual tech controls are escalating, yet commercial dependence persists; a fresh flashpoint ahead of any Trump–Xi meeting.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (21 Oct) · Semafor Flagship (20 Oct) · Rest of World

• Airfares set for their “biggest increase in history”. Ryanair’s Michael O’Leary expects European fares to rise up to 20% next year as fuel hedges roll off and consolidation cuts seats. Ryanair is also cutting 20 Belgian routes this winter after the national aviation tax rises from €2 to €7 in January.

Why it matters: Higher fuel costs are passing through to services inflation and tourism economies such as Italy and Spain.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (21 Oct) · Politico Playbook Capitals

• Software M&A thaws. Thoma Bravo’s Orlando Bravo says software’s death “has been greatly exaggerated”, though it is “a really, really hard time to buy and sell a company.” Deals are resuming: Schneider’s $23bn PTC acquisition and a $6bn Varonis takeover.

Why it matters: A reopening LBO market would support private-equity exits and leveraged-loan supply after a frozen year.

Sources: Semafor Business

• Private markets get stickier. Goldman, Evercore and others are pitching “evergreen” continuation vehicles that let PE firms hold assets indefinitely; separately, Bloomberg found Mark Walter’s insurers rolled “short-term” loans to his own firms for years, cutting capital charges to as little as 0.39%.

Why it matters: Permanent capital and regulatory arbitrage in insurers make private-credit risk harder to see — a concern for regulators as the AI debt pile grows.

Sources: Bloomberg Money Stuff

• Waymo goes big; consumer giants unwind. Waymo plans to grow from 4,000 to 20,000 robotaxis by end-2027, funded by $5bn of debt without an explicit Alphabet backstop. Unilever is selling Welly, Pepsi’s $2bn Poppi is underperforming and Coca-Cola wants to unwind Costa.

Why it matters: Capital is rotating toward AI-adjacent bets while legacy brands retreat from costly “street-cred” acquisitions.

Sources: Semafor Business

World

• Rubio courts India’s refiners. Calling India a “refining superpower,” Marco Rubio tours Reliance’s refinery (the world’s largest) on Wednesday to push it to process more Venezuelan crude, after Washington waived sanctions on buyers of Russian oil and gas.

Why it matters: Washington is prioritising pump prices before the midterms over sanctions pressure on Moscow.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (21 Oct)

• Israel votes next week. Confidence in election integrity fell to an eight-year low of 56% (Gallup); Netanyahu trails a former general in most polls, and Itamar Ben-Gvir’s party is gaining with young urban voters. Analysts expect no fundamental policy shift whoever wins.

Why it matters: Continuity on Iran and Gaza policy is likely, keeping regional escalation risk elevated.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (21 Oct) · Reuters Daily Briefing · Polymarket Polygraph

• Brazil’s runoff is a toss-up. Flávio Bolsonaro’s lead over Lula has shrunk to 0.6 points before Sunday’s vote; the winner will appoint at least four Supreme Court justices. Phone theft has become a top campaign issue.

Why it matters: The result will shape fiscal policy, the real and Latin America’s largest economy for years.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (20 Oct) · Semafor Flagship (21 Oct)

• China’s jobs strain and sea standoffs. Youth unemployment reached almost 19% in August and many jobless Chinese stage “fake commutes”; Beijing is set to become super-aged within a decade. China’s navy shadowed a US warship near a disputed South China Sea shoal, and Taiwan reported a standoff with a Chinese vessel.

Why it matters: Weak domestic demand keeps China dependent on exports, sharpening trade frictions as maritime tensions rise.

Sources: Semafor Flagship (20 Oct) · Reuters Daily Briefing

• Alberta stays in Canada; South Africa hedges. Albertans voted to remain in Canada in a first-ever separation referendum, a win for Mark Carney amid the US trade war. South Africa’s president warned the country may lose US duty-free access when the deal is renegotiated in 2028 and is courting China and India.

Why it matters: US trade pressure is pushing allies and partners to diversify, eroding Washington’s economic leverage.

Sources: Reuters Daily Briefing · Semafor Flagship (20 Oct)

• The age of “heat storms”. A Reuters analysis finds supersized heat waves covering at least 1% of the globe have risen from about five a year in the 1960s to about 100 a year this decade.

Why it matters: More frequent extreme heat raises costs for insurers, agriculture, power grids and tourism.

Sources: Reuters Daily Briefing

Europe

EU passes the Ukraine hat to Canada, the UK, Norway and Japan

Brussels will use summits with Canada (29–30 Oct) and the UK (20 Nov) to press allies for more money for Kyiv, whose additional 2027 needs the last EU summit put at €70bn; Ottawa and Oslo have responded positively, and Japan is being approached via the G7/G20. Officials say more partner money comes before thornier options such as another EU loan or frozen Russian assets, which Belgium still resists.

Why it matters: With US support unreliable, Europe must close Ukraine’s funding gap; failure would revive the frozen-assets fight and strain EU budgets.

Sources: Politico Brussels Playbook

Italy: soldiers for Milan, and a northern revolt over EU budget cuts

Meloni’s cabinet met to approve an emergency decree deploying 500 soldiers in Milan after violent weekend protests, with measures under consideration including preventive detention of minors and curbs on gatherings. Meanwhile Lombardy’s Attilio Fontana, joined by Piedmont, Liguria and Veneto, attacks the Commission’s 2028–34 budget, which shifts money from cohesion to defence and competitiveness and lets capitals split a single pot; Lombardy could lose €1bn, while €218bn is ring-fenced only for poorer regions.

Why it matters: Unrest is spreading from France to Italy, and the budget fight pits Italy’s economic engine against Rome and Brussels as European borrowing costs climb.

Sources: Politico Playbook Capitals

UK–EU: Barnier tells Burnham to show he’s serious

After PM Andy Burnham reopened the Brexit debate (options up to rejoining), Michel Barnier said London must clarify its intentions and debate them in Parliament; the 20 Nov summit covers food trade, carbon markets and youth mobility. Beijing threatened retaliation if the UK aligns its EV tariffs with the EU’s.

Why it matters: Closer UK–EU ties could lift trade, but alignment carries a China cost, and Labour is split on rejoining.

Sources: Politico Playbook Capitals · Politico Brussels Playbook

Around the capitals

Romania’s parliament installed Luca Niculescu as PM after three failed attempts, with a six-month deadline for a lasting deal. Spain’s PP is negotiating joint Senate lists with Vox before the 29 Nov election. Poland’s parliament approved bills loosening abortion rules, which President Nawrocki could veto. The Patriots for Europe became Parliament’s third-largest group (85 MEPs), and EU ambassadors discuss delaying the methane regulation’s January 2027 importer deadline.

Why it matters: The right is gaining institutional weight across Europe just as climate rules face rollback pressure.
